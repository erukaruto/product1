How we define MRR, and why the definition matters

Three teams, three spreadsheets, three different numbers. Here is the definition Beacon uses, the edge cases it handles, and how to explain it to your board.

PS Priya SethiData engineering · Oct 20, 2026 · 6 min read
On this page The problem Our definition Edge cases A worked example Explaining it to your board

The problem

Ask finance, product and support for last month's revenue and you will often get three answers. Finance counts what was invoiced. Product counts active plans. Support counts the accounts they talk to. None of them is wrong, but they can't all be MRR.

When the numbers disagree, every meeting starts with a debate about which one to trust. That is the cost we wanted to remove.

Our definition

MRR is the sum of the normalized monthly price of every active subscription on the last day of the month. "Normalized" means a yearly plan counts as one twelfth of its price each month.

  • Discounts are subtracted for as long as they apply.
  • Taxes, one-off fees and usage overages are left out.
  • Trials count as zero until the first successful payment.
  • Past-due subscriptions stay in MRR until they are canceled.
Why keep past-due accounts?

Most failed payments recover within a week. Removing them right away makes MRR jump up and down for reasons that have nothing to do with demand.

Edge cases

The definition is short. The edge cases are where teams disagree:

  1. Mid-month upgrades count from the day they happen, so the month-end number reflects the new plan.
  2. Pauses count as zero for the paused months, then return as reactivation, not new MRR.
  3. Refunds don't change MRR. They change cash, which is a different report.

A worked example

Explaining it to your board

"We count what customers are committed to pay each month, after discounts, before tax."

That one sentence covers most questions. When someone asks about an edge case, show the movement table above. It answers "where did the number come from" without a spreadsheet.
